Try independently, compare with a partner, then explain why your method works. Write complete reasons. These are practice activities, not hand-in questions.

Activity 1

Let S={m∈Z≥0 : m>17/4}. List four elements and find its least element. Explain why well-ordering applies. Contrast this with T={x∈R : x>17/4}: does T have a least element?

Activity 2

Find integers q,r such that (a) −29=6q+r with 0≤r<6; (b) 29=(−6)q+r with 0≤r<6. In (a), a student writes q=−4,r=−5. Is the equation correct? Is this the required division-algorithm answer? If the remainder restriction is removed, exhibit two answers.

Activity 3

Suppose −29=6q+r=6q′+r′, where q,q′,r,r′ are integers and 0≤r,r′<6. Prove the two representations must agree. Your proof must use both the equality and the remainder bounds.

Activity 4

(a) Prove that if 4 divides u and 4 divides v, then 4 divides 3u−2v. (b) A student claims that 8|3u−2v follows too. Decide by proof or counterexample. (c) List the positive common divisors of 18 and 30 and identify their gcd.

Activity 5

You are given 6=2·18−30. Use it to prove that every common divisor of 18 and 30 divides 6. Then explain why it certifies gcd(18,30)=6. Finally, can integers x,y satisfy 18x+30y=9?

Activity 6

Without notes: (1) Find q,r for 22=(−5)q+r with 0≤r<5. (2) State the two facts needed to certify that a positive integer g is gcd(a,b). (3) Name one hypothesis of well-ordering that you must check.
